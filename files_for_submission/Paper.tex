\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Competitive games have become a major source of large-scale behavioral
data, enabling researchers to study strategic decision-making, skill
development, and population-level trends using observational logs at
scales unheard of compared to controlled studies
\citep{edelmann2020css, watts2014css}. Chess is one of the most famous
competitive games in the world, and the relatively recent growth of
online platforms has created datasets with millions to billions of games
played under standardized rules and well-defined competitive contexts
(time controls, ratings, game outcomes, etc.)
\citep{watts2014css, lichessDatabase}. One of the most widely used
sources is the Lichess Open Database, which provides public monthly
exports of rated games played on lichess.org in PGN format
\citep{lichessDatabase, lichessDatabaseGitHub}. These exports make it
possible to study how strategy and performance vary across skill levels
and time controls in a modern, online environment.

This project intends to use Lichess game data to investigate
contemporary metagame behavior and player development. Here, `metagame'
refers to the population-level distribution of strategic choices and
styles observed in practice -- such as opening families, risk
preferences, and game outcomes -- within a defined time window and
player population. In addition to describing how strategic behavior
differs by rating and time control, the project will attempt to connect
these strategic signatures to measures of player progression over time.
Because chess is a repeatedly played, fully competitive setting with a
mature rating infrastructure, it offers a natural testbed for
quantifying skill and comparing behavior across different players
\citep{lichessRatingSystems, glickmanGlicko2Desc}.

A central component of modern chess analytics is the rating system used
to summarize player strength. The ELO rating system -- a system
originally developed for chess -- models player performance
probabilistically, and remains a foundational reference point for
interpreting skill differences and expected outcomes in competitive
settings \citep{elo1978rating, fideElo2025}. Lichess uses a modified
version of this system called `Glicko-2', which, in addition to rating,
tracks uncertainty via rating deviation and volatility
\citep{lichessRatingSystems, glickmanGlicko2Desc}. Ratings are
especially useful for observational studies because they provide a
consistent scale by which to stratify players and construct longitudinal
measures of improvement \citep{glickmanGlicko2Desc}.

Overall, this project attempts to marry data science, computational
social science, and strategic behavior: It treats online chess as a
real-world environment where strategic choices and performance outcomes
can be measured at scale and subsequently analyzed using statistical
learning methods \citep{edelmann2020css, watts2014css}.

\subsection{Background}\label{sec-background}

A growing research literature uses large online chess datasets to study
human performance, decision-making, and strategic patterns. For example,
Chowdhary et al.~leverage an online chess dataset at the scale of over
100 million games to quantify performance dynamics and career-like
trajectories, illustrating how large game archives support
population-level behavioral measurement across skill levels
\citep{chowdhary2023performance}.

One important line of work focuses on time usage and decision quality
under time constraints. Online chess platforms record both move
sequences and clock information, which allows researchers to study how
players allocate thinking time and how time pressure shapes decisions.
Russek et al.~analyze millions of Lichess games to relate move times to
the estimated value of additional computation, and show that time
allocation patterns vary systematically with playing strength and time
control \citep{russek2025thinking}. These findings support the idea that
online chess is a rich setting for studying bounded rationality and
performance under constraints, especially when comparing bullet, blitz,
rapid, and classical formats \citep{russek2025thinking}.

Chess opening choice is also an active research topic. Because openings
are discrete, labelable strategic choices that occur early in every
game, they are well suited to population-level analysis. De Marzo and
Servedio use online chess community data to quantify opening similarity
and complexity, including representing openings in network form and
studying how opening-related structure can be used to forecast future
opening adoption \citep{demarzo2023openings}. This motivates analyzing
metagame development in terms of shifting opening popularity and success
rates across time, player skill, and time controls
\citep{demarzo2023openings, chowdhary2023performance}.

Finally, there is an emerging interest in identifying player styles or
archetypes from game logs. While traditional chess analysis often
focuses on engine evaluation of positions, a complementary approach is
to extract behavioral features from move sequences and outcomes (e.g.,
game length, opening selection, and other style proxies) and use
statistical learning to characterize player types. For instance,
McIlroy-Young et al.~demonstrate that models trained on move sequences
can capture individual-specific signatures (``style'') in chess
decisions, providing evidence that player behavior is meaningfully
distinguishable from game records alone
\citep{mcilroyyoung2021stylometry}. This creates a bridge between
strategic analysis (what players choose to do) and longitudinal outcomes
(how players improve or persist over time), which aligns closely with
the goals of this project.

Taken together, existing work suggests that large-scale online chess
datasets support three relevant kinds of analysis: (1) descriptive
metagame analysis of strategic choices across populations, (2) modeling
how constraints like time pressure affect behavior and outcomes, and (3)
data-driven characterization of player types and their evolution
\citep{chowdhary2023performance, russek2025thinking, demarzo2023openings, mcilroyyoung2021stylometry}.
This project intends to build on those themes by using Lichess data to
analyze modern opening/style patterns and connect them to measures of
player performance and development over time.

\subsection{Source}\label{sec-source}

Describe where this data comes from, why it was collected, who collected
it, and how it was collected. A Citation may be useful here.

Lichess is the largest open-source chess server is the world, and
releases free, monthly exports of their game data under the Creative
Commons CCO license \citep{lichessDatabase}. The site requires a log of
the game data in order to function while a game is being played, but
holds onto it after the fact to allow users to analyze their games after
playing them. Though Lichess offers a powerful API, I elected to
download data directly from their website in \texttt{.pgn.zst} format.
The PGN (portable game notation) format is a plaintext format for chess
games, containing moves, headers like players, date, and event, and even
optional annotation. The \texttt{.zst} file extension (sometimes also
written as \texttt{zstd}) represents Zstandard compression, which is a
modern compressor similar to gzip or xz that's commonly used because
it's fast and compresses well; think of it as a variation on a ZIP or
RAR file. Zstandard compression can be expected to shrink PGN files by 5
times on its faster, less-efficient settings, and even more on it's
slower ones.

For the purposes of this project, I downloaded 24 months of data
spanning from January 2024 to December 2025, amounting to 677 gigabytes
of raw data when compressed. Obviously, nearly 700 gigabytes of
compressed data -- 3\textasciitilde4 terabytes of uncompressed PGN data
-- is an insane amount to try to work with, capstone project or not. I
have a powerful home computer, but that kind of scale requires something
closer to a high-end server than a personal computer; it won't fly.
Instead of tackling the problem head-on, I constructed a hybrid R/Python
pipeline that downloads the data, deterministically samples and parses
it into small chunks (in the form of parquet files), and then builds a
selection of smaller, ready-for-analysis datasets from those chunks.
Individually, no one dataset has the 20+ variables required in the
project outline, but since I've built multiple datasets for multiple
different types of analysis, they collectively meet that benchmark

The download and parsing section of the pipeline handles the following:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Reads a config file \texttt{pipeline.yml} that controls the scope and
  parameters of the pipeline's operation.
\item
  Downloads monthly Lichess `standard rated' PGN archives for each month
  specified in the YAML.
\item
  Decompresses each Zstandard file as a stream, scanning games
  one-by-one so it doesn't run into RAM bottlenecks.
\item
  Parses game headers first to decide whether a game is relevant before
  doing any heavy work (not all games are actually getting sampled, so
  it skips over the ones not being used).

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Extracts core metadata (players, ratings, time control, result,
    termination, opening, timestamps).
  \item
    Buckets each game into blitz/rapid/classical using the Lichess
    time-control convention.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Applies filtering rules from the YAML, which in this case are:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Only include blitz/rapid/classical time controls.
  \item
    Only include games from players in the 800--2600 rating range.
  \item
    Drops unwanted terminations (aborted games).
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Uses deterministic hash-based sampling to create reproducible subsets
  without needing to store the whole population:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    A meta sample keyed by \texttt{game\_id} for population/metagame
    summaries, at a rate of 5/500 games.
  \item
    A player cohort keyed by \texttt{username} for longitudinal/player
    tracking at a rate of 3/500 players.
  \item
    A moves sample keyed by \texttt{game\_id} for clock/time-per-move
    data at a rate of 5/10000 moves.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Deterministically anonymizes usernames based on a stable, salted hash.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Cohort selection is computed on the real username before
    anonymization, so it remains reproducible.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Writes month-partitioned parquet outputs for game-level data.
\end{itemize}

The dataset-building portion of the pipeline takes those outputs and,
using DuckDB to read and write parquet files on the disk, builds seven
datasets from them:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Player-level longitudinal summary tables from \texttt{games\_cohort}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \texttt{player\_month} (\texttt{pm}): player * month, 249,761
    observations.
  \item
    \texttt{player\_month\_tc} (\texttt{pm\_tc}): player * month * time
    control, 320,869 observations.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Player-opening `repertoire' table from \texttt{games\_cohort}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \texttt{player\_opening\_month\_tc} (\texttt{pom\_tc}): player *
    month * time control * opening, 7,167,580 observations.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Metagame tables from \texttt{games\_meta}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \texttt{meta\_month\_tc} (\texttt{mm\_tc}): month * time control, 72
    observations.
  \item
    \texttt{meta\_opening\_month\_tc} (\texttt{mom\_tc}): month * time
    control * opening, 130,672 observations.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Time-usage tables from \texttt{moves\_meta}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \texttt{moves\_month\_tc} (\texttt{mvm\_tc}) month * time control,
    72 observations.
  \item
    \texttt{player\_time\_month\_tc} (\texttt{ptm\_tc}): player * month
    * time control, 1,038,050 observations.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The pipeline logs session and version info for both R and Python and
records checksums for downloaded raw files to help with reproducibility.

\section{Exploratory Data Analysis}\label{sec-eda}

Figure~\ref{fig-coverage}, the first and most important of the EDA
plots, shows the number of observations the parser sampled per
player-month, colored by time control.\footnote{Throughout, `rows' refer
  to aggregated observation units (e.g., player-month-time-control or
  opening-month-time-control records), whereas `games' refer to the
  underlying sampled matches contributing to those aggregates. A change
  in row counts therefore reflects changes in the breadth of observed
  groups, not necessarily a change in total game volume.} It highlights
a fundamental flaw in my parsing method--- one that I failed to consider
until seeing it visualized. See, while the parser filters the raw data
down to a smaller subset of time controls and rating ranges, it does
nothing to ensure that there's an equal distribution of games for given
time controls or players of given ratings. Rather, it simply samples in
a pseudo-random manner. This means that the distribution of certain
variables in the cleaned dataset will reflect the true distribution of
those variables among all games stored in the Lichess database. In more
straightforward terms, popular time controls like blitz are played more
often than less-popular ones like classical, and that relationship is
mirrored in the data, as evidenced in Figure~\ref{fig-coverage}.

This finding isn't the end of the world, but it \emph{is} extremely
important to control for. It means that some `missingness' will be
structural rather than truly random, and that there will be a general
imbalance across time controls, ratings, and months. It also justifies
restricting certain analyses to blitz/rapid (or else collapsing them
entirely), adding minimum-support thresholds to ensure there's enough
data, and treating month as a potential confound only when coverage is
stable enough; buckets with adequate support will be emphasized, and
low-support buckets should be treated as exploratory only.

\caption{\label{fig-coverage}Proportion of observations per time control

\end{figure}%

Figure~\ref{fig-reliability} and Figure~\ref{fig-panel-depth} display
two different kinds of unreliability inherent to player-month: the

necessarily consecutive). For Figure~\ref{fig-reliability}, many
player-months contain few games, making outcomes like win rate or
average rating noisy at the unit level, justifying a minimum-games
filter for player-month analyses, weighting player-month observations by
games in aggregated models, and preferring aggregated measures such as
bins over raw point clouds; game count should be treated as a
precision/weighting signal.

Pictured in Figure~\ref{fig-delta-rating} is without contest the single
most perfect Gaussian distribution I have ever seen in a real-world
dataset. This is not a parsing mistake, nor the result of some sort of
transformation, but a natural consequence of the Glicko-2 rating system
employed by Lichess. Both Glicko-2 and its predecessor Glicko assume
that player skill -- both for individuals \emph{and} for a given
population -- follows a normal distribution. Because of that,
implementations of Glicko systems almost always result in
normally-distributed skill ratings, provided the player count is high
enough.\footnote{It's important to note that Glicko-based systems don't
  `force' ratings to conform to a normal distribution; it's possible for
  them to fall into any form, but the way the system is designed tends
  to converge towards normality in most cases.} Practically, this means
that \(\Delta\) rating should be treated as a noisy outcome requiring
careful aggregation, preferring binned summaries over unconstrained
smoothing. It also encourages caution when interpreting
statistically-significant-yet-tiny effects, as small, downright
impractical or meaningless outcomes can still read as significant when
dealing with a distribution like this.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{Paper_files/figure-pdf/fig-delta-rating-1.pdf}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-delta-rating}Rating change is centered near zero
with roughly symmetric dispersion. Most month-to-month changes are
small, so effect sizes will likely be modest.}

\end{figure}%

Unsurprisingly, Figure~\ref{fig-volatility} shows that the variance in
players' month-to-moth ratings decreases with the number of games played
in a given month. Importantly, the figure is log-transformed; though the
plot visualizes a linear relationship, the first hundred games or so are
comparatively more impactful than the next thousand. This is a
relationship that I would expect to see with any rating system -- law of
large numbers and all -- but was something I was expecting from Glicko-2
in particular. On top of rating, Glicko systems also measure
uncertainty, which is a Bayesian-style measure of how confident it is in
a player's rating. Because uncertainty always reduces as more games are
played, it makes sense that this relationship is so clearly present in
the data. This figure helps reinforce the need for minimum-games filters
(which will reduce heteroskedastic noise) and shows that weighting by
games is defensible as precision weighting. It also supports
longer-horizon \(\Delta\) (over multiple months instead of just one) as
a way to increase robustness because it averages noise.

\section{Methodology}\label{methodology}

At the core of this project are three themes, as listed in the
Section~\ref{sec-background}: (1) descriptive metagame dynamics, (2) how
time constraints relate to behavior and performance, and (3)
characterization of player types or styles. The analysis is strictly
non-causal; models are used for description, association, and prediction
under out-of-sample evaluation.

When splitting the data into train and test sets, all predictive
modeling uses forward-in-time splits to avoid data leakage. This means
that the training set contains earlier months in the sample window,
while the validation/test set are a contiguous block of later months
that were held out from the training data. This split is applied at the
month level so that predictors from any given month are never trained
using information from future months. For player progression tasks,
where the target is a future rating change, using time-forward splits is
essential for keeping evaluation realistic.

When appropriate, models are additionally checked for robustness under
time-control and rating-bin stratification, to ensure performance isn't
driven by a single skill or time-control band.

\subsection{Data Tranformations}\label{data-tranformations}

Transformations serve to stabilize noisy estimates, make quantities
comparable across players and contexts, and construct interpretable
features aligned with all three research topics. Different
transformations are applied for different purposes.

\subsubsection{Aggregation-Level Alignment}\label{sec-aggregation}

Rather than a single, monolithic dataset, this project handles several
datasets aligned to differing observational units. Strictly speaking,
this alignment occurs during the data pipeline itself rather than a
formal, pre-modeling cleaning step but it's important to note
nonetheless. Broadly, the observational units for each table can be
separated into four categories: metagame (population-level games),
player outcomes (player-level games), time usage, and opening
repertoire. This, however, is an oversimplification; please refer to
Section~\ref{sec-data} for more detail.

\subsubsection{Binning and
Standardization}\label{binning-and-standardization}

To support nonparametric EDA and stable estimation, rating is often
binned for plots and stratified summaries--- several of which have
already been featured in Section~\ref{sec-eda}. For clustering (player
type/style discovery), features are standardized using z-scores so that
variables measured on different scales (e.g., seconds, proportions,
counts) contribute in a comparable manner.

\subsubsection{Exposure Weighting and Time-Control-Collapsed
Quantities}\label{exposure-weighting-and-time-control-collapsed-quantities}

As displayed by Figure~\ref{fig-coverage} in Section~\ref{sec-eda}, in
some contexts it's justifiable to collapse observations across time
controls, particularly where classical is concerned. When a model needs
`overall' player-month measures rather than those specific to a given
time control, overall rating is constructed as a games-weighted average
of \texttt{avg\_rating} across time controls. Similarly, overall
time-trouble rate becomes an average of \texttt{low\_time\_rate} across
time controls, weighted by move rows. This prevents the volume disparity
between time controls from disproportionately influencing aggregated
quantities.

\subsubsection{Reliability Thresholds}\label{reliability-thresholds}

Many variables are rates estimated from finite samples. Often, these
sample sizes are extremely small, increasing volatility. In order to
reduce such noise, minimum thresholds for observation count may be
applied; player-month observations may require a minimum number of games
per player, time-use observations a number of move-rows, and
opening-level estimates a number of games per group. This filtering may
still be applied even if the model or models being fit don't explicitly
require it to function (or as part of a fundamental model assumption) to
ensure that modeled signals are not dominated by small-sample
volatility.

\subsubsection{Derived Features}\label{derived-features}

A set of interpetable style features is constructed from player-opening
and time-use tables for use in type discovery and player-progression
models. Similarly to Section~\ref{sec-aggregation}, some of these
features were created during the data pipeline itself, while others are
derived directly from them. These include:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Repertoire breath, measured by \texttt{opening\_diversity}.
\item
  Repertoire concentration, computed as a share of games in the
  most-played opening from \texttt{player\_opening\_month}.
\item
  Opening switching, indicated by whether \texttt{top\_opening} differs
  from the previous month.
\item
  Repertoire churn, measured jointly as:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The distance between consecutive-month opening-share vectors.
  \item
    The share of games in brand new openings relative to the month
    prior.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Time-pressure behavior, based on a combination of
  \texttt{low\_time\_rate} plus summaries like
  \texttt{avg\_time\_spent}, \texttt{p90\_time\_spent}, and
  \texttt{avg\_time\_opening}.
\end{itemize}

For more information on pre-existing features, see
Section~\ref{sec-data}.

\subsubsection{Missingness}\label{missingness}

Finally, phase-specific time metrics are often missing for structural
reasons. Almost always, the phase missing is endgame, as many games
never reach a computer-defined endgame due to resignation or blundered,
early losses. Models that use phase variables either include explicit
missingness handling or analyze phases separately with appropriate
inclusion rules.

\subsection{Types of Models}\label{types-of-models}

Because this project aims to explore a variety of questions by modeling
on multiple datasets, strictly categorizing models by family makes
little sense.\footnote{Here, I define ``model family'' as sets of models
  operating under similar principles and fundamental assumptions (e.g.,
  general linear models, tree-based models, etc.), whereas ``model
  category'' refers to the five groupings of models I specifically
  define for this paper.} Were there a single theme and associated
dataset, doing so would make it easy to illustrate exactly how each type
of model contributes to the project's singular goal. In this case,
however, I think it more appropriate to categorize models by their goal;
to define them by the questions they intend to answer and the methods
they'll use in seeking them.\footnote{It should be noted that this form
  of model categorization allows for slight overlap between model
  families; some families may be used by multiple categories of model.}

\subsubsection{Metagame Dynamics}\label{sec-metagame-dynamics}

The goal of metagame models are to quantify how aggregate metagame
metrics vary across factors such as time and format. The primary
observational unit is \texttt{meta\_opening\_month\_tc}, while response
variables include \texttt{share} and outcome rates (e.g.,
\texttt{white\_win\_rate}). The model families which fall under this
category include generalized additive models (GAM) and weighted linear
models (WLM), as well as multinomial models.

GAM and WLM models are useful because they produce simple, easy-to-read
trend estimates. The incorporation of weights let high-support
opening-month cells impact the fit more than small, noisy cells, and
GAM-style smoothing allows for nonlinear time trends without forcing a
specific form. Both are strong at tracking a small set of common
openings over time (which, given the EDA, is very relevant \#ref) while
remaining easy to justify and visualize. Importantly, because opening
shares are bounded and compositional, these models can only ever
approximate the true structure of opening share, meaning that results
will necessarily be biased or even potentially spurious.

Multinomial models exist to solve the problem of compositional
variables. By modeling on log-odds and log-ratios, they avoid predicting
impossible shares (\(\notin 0 < x < 1\)); in essence, they're capable of
implicitly understanding that if one opening goes up, others must go
down. This provides interpretable effects for opening adoption relative
to a specific baseline, meaning they're best when relative substitution
between openings is of import. Multinomial models tend to work better
when modeling a large portion of a distribution (rather than just the
top k), but as a consequence are more complicated to both implement and
interpret.

Combined, these models attempt to ask questions about the state of the
metagame -- the Lichess `ecosystem' -- and its evolution rather than
just fitting adoption curves.

\subsubsection{Opening Popularity}\label{opening-popularity}

The goal of opening-popularity models is to describe how opening
preferences differ by player skill and time control. You'll notice that
this goal shares substantial overlap with that of metagame models, and
indeed, opening popularity is a subset of metagame dynamics. The core
difference between the two is that opening-popularity models exclusively
target the adoption rate of openings at the expense of all other
metagame descriptors; they care not for the diversity of the opening
pool, or how outcome rates, game length, and rating gaps shift, for
instance. While both use \texttt{meta\_opening\_month\_tc} as an
observational unit, popularity models prefer to treat each opening
separately (top-k) or as a compositional set focused on \texttt{share}.
Models in this category include multinomial models, count models, and
weighted regression.

Contrary to the metagame category, multinomial models are front and
center for opening popularity because the target is explicitly
compositional. Metagame models seek to observe large-scale patterns more
than anything, so in their case it's possible to get away with using
multinomials as a backup for increased accuracy, but when it comes to
opening popularity there's no real excuse. Multinomial choice models
build player-game as a categorical choice, producing probabilities of
choosing each opening as a function of things like rating and time
control. They're computationally heavy, but are worth the price if they
have access to enough data.

Count models such as poisson and negative binomial are a simpler answer
to the same problem, treating observed opening use as counts out of a
total number of games. By applying an offset (e.g., taking the log of
total games), they can effectively model rates while remaining in count
space. Negative binomial can handle overdispersion, which is common in
count data. Their main advantage over multinomial models is their
ability to produce interpretable, multiplicative effects, or rate
ratios, which allows for another angle of analysis.

Finally, weighted regression on \texttt{share} takes the `simple is
best' philosophy to an extreme. Just as in the prior section, they don't
enforce composition, meaning they're liable to misbehave at the
boundaries if used for extrapolation; they can't replace multinomial or
count models and only serve as an approximation. They remain for much
the same reasons as before: they're easy to implement, easy to explain,
and good when the only goal is a half-decent descriptive model focused
on top openings. Weighted regression certainly won't be the star of the
show, but it's such a simple addition that it costs nothing to include.

Together, these models seek to hone in on opening popularity with a
higher degree of specificity than the metagame models, each through
varying levels of complexity.

\subsubsection{Time-Use}\label{time-use}

Time use models attempt to characterize the way time allocation varies
across skill and time control. Observational units are
\texttt{player\_time\_month\_tc} for player-level behavior and
\texttt{moves\_month\_tc} for macro behavior. Response variables include
time spent, time management, pressure, and phase allocation, while
predictors primarily consist of \texttt{avg\_rating} and
\texttt{tc\_bucket}. Three related pairs of models populate this
category: additive- and interaction regression, robust- and quantile
regression, and beta- and logistic-style modeling.

Additive regression and interaction regression provide a clean
separation between skill effects and constraint effects. Additive
regression assumes each predictor contributes its own effect, and that
those effects simply add up, best for when predictors are believed to
act independently of one another. They also tend to be a substitute for
interaction regression when there's not enough data. Interaction
regression, as the name implies, allows the effect of one predictor to
depend on the level of another predictor. Both are relatively easy to
interpret.

Robust regression and quantile regression are useful in situations when
ordinary least squares (OLS) is a poor fit. Notably, both solve
different problems. Robust regression still estimates the conditional
mean like OLS, but makes it less sensitive to outliers, whereas quantile
regression breaks the mold by estimating a conditional quantile (e.g.,
median, 90th percentile, etc.) rather than the mean. As a consequence,
they must also be interpreted slightly differently, as they ask how
predictors shift a percentile rather than the mean. They're valuable for
modeling time use because time-related variables often present heavy
tails, which these models are better equipped to handle.

Beta and logistic modeling are along for the ride because of a specific
problem. Namely, \texttt{low\_time\_rate} is bounded and almost always
piles up at 0. Both beta and logit-style approaches keep predictions
bounded in {[}0, 1{]}, handling proportion structure better than OLS,
the core difference between the two being that logistic modeling uses a
binary response (or a count of successes out of a total number of
trials), while beta modeling uses a continuous proportion between 0 and
1. By binning low-time rate, it's possible to produce stable summaries
that avoid smoothing failures, making for a more-easily-defensible model

Together, these models thoroughly explore several different methods of
time-use analysis. The reason this section contains three pairs of
complimentary models rather than just the `better' of each pair is that
the EDA did not produce conclusive enough evidence to justify jumping
straight to a smaller subset of model options; the data is complex
enough that a simple visual inspection was insufficient for ruling out
one choice or the other, and so the most reliable solution is to test
them all.

\subsubsection{Player-Progression
Models}\label{player-progression-models}

Player-progression models are straightforward: They wish to relate a
player's current-month behavior to future rating change. The purpose of
this category is simple, asking if it's possible to isolate certain
factors that prove to have a marked effect on a player's growth---
positive \emph{or} negative. The baseline observational unit for these
models is \texttt{player\_month}, which can be stratified by time
control (\texttt{player\_month\_tc}) or joined to other tables for
additional information (e.g., \texttt{player\_time\_month\_tc} or
\texttt{player\_opening\_month\_tc}). The response variables are derived
from \texttt{avg\_rating} and all represent \(\Delta rating\) in some
form. Horizon spans may differ (i.e., rating change over spans greater
than a single month), and constructing a stability model using
\(|\Delta|\) is also possible. Predictors include the number of games
and moves played during a given timespan, style proxies, and time-use
features, controlled by baseline rating and other fixed effects.
Comprising this category are model families such as regularized linear
models, mixed-/fixed effects models, and tree-based regression.

Regularized linear models (ridge, lasso, elastic net) help account for
the fact that this category's predictors are correlated. Regularization
stabilizes these estimates. Ridge regression is good for prediction
under multicollinearity, and lasso, though perhaps less relevant here is
good for feature selection. Elastic net is a hybrid of the two. As a
whole, they should provide a strong baseline predictive model without
sacrificing interpretability.

Fixed effects models and mixed (random) effects models are good at
handling longitudinal data, though they differ in how they handle
time-invariant predictors. The former controls for time-invariant
differences without attempting to predict their effects, while the
latter tries to estimate said effects. Fixed effects push towards
within-player identification, while mixed effects will pool players to
improve stability. Crucially, mixed effects models assume that unit
intercepts are uncorrelated to the regressors, which can't be reasonably
assumed with this data. Fixed effects models are likely to be the better
performing of the two (if non-fixed models even pass muster at all),
though the implementation of something like a correlated random effects
(Mundlak) model might help bridge the gap.

Tree-based regression both naturally captures nonlinearities and
thresholds in the data and automatically handles interactions without
explicit specification. They're best for exploratory prediction and
detecting complex feature relationships. As with all tree-based models,
this comes at the cost of interpretability; they require interpretation
tools such as feature importance, partial dependence plots, individual
conditional expectation plots, et cetera, and even then struggle to
match the interpretability of more-straightforward models.

Together, these models account for the varying facets of longitudinal
player-level modeling in order to identify the best angle to attack the
problem from.

\subsubsection{Player Categorization}\label{player-categorization}

As the name implies, player-categorization models want to categorize
players. More specifically, they seek to identify clusters using
behavioral features, stratifying by rating and format, and observing for
stability. This is by far the most nebulous of the model categories
because they're primarily exploratory in nature; rather than beginning
with pre-defined player archetypes and asking how their performance
differs, these models ask which style clusters (if any) can be
identified in the first place. The most straightforward observational
unit is a join between \texttt{player\_month\_tc},
\texttt{player\_time\_month\_tc}, and repertoire-derived features, and
inputs include performance, outcomes, repertoire breadth, time use, and
general context. The core model families in this category are
dimensionality reduction and clustering, though additional modeling
becomes possible after playstyle has been categorized.

Dimensionality reduction methods such as principal component analysis
(PCA) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) provide
low-dimensional representation. PCA yields interpretable axes and helps
with noise reduction, whereas UMAP is useful for visual separations and
nonlinear manifolds. PCA is a linear method which tries to preserve
large-scale structure, while UMAP is a nonlinear method prefers
preserving cluster separation. Dimensionality reduction is good at for
summarizing multi-feature space \emph{before} clustering or
visualization, which should prove helpful in the case of a very broad
`style space'.

Clustering methods (e.g., k-means, hierarchical, GMM) are potentially
capable of turning continuous player-behavior into discrete, describable
archetypes. K-means clustering is fast, but requires tuning towards an
unknown k. Hierarchical clustering is tree-based and doesn't require a
known k, but requires a dendrogram for interpretation, and Gaussian
mixture models (GMM) creates clusters of varying sizes with associated
membership probabilities, which is useful if player archetypes overlap.

Though perhaps not within the scope of this project, dimensionality
reduction and clustering are notable as a potential method of creating
discrete types for downstream modeling. Once each player-month has a
type, it becomes possible to study stability and movement between types
or perform supervised follow-up using type labels (e.g., as predictors
of progression).

Together, these models explore the bounds of player-archetype/playstyle
categorization.

\subsection{Model Selection}\label{model-selection}

Because this project does not intend to settle on a single, final,
`best' model, I have not outlined an exact process for model selection.
Rather, the sheer quantity and breadth of models tested naturally pushes
toward a broader set of more-general rules to follow. The rules I have
defined are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Start with defensible baselines.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Make the simplest possible model (or set of models) in a given
    category that fulfill reasonable standards for transparency,
    methodology, and fulfilled model assumptions.
  \item
    Compare increasingly-complex models to their baseline counterparts
    when assessing their worth.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Prefer simpler models when performance is comparable.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    When two models perform similarly, the one with fewer assumptions
    and clearer interpretation is preferable for reporting.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Check stability to thresholds and stratification.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Key results must be checked for sensitivity to minimum thresholds
    and to whether analyses are stratified by time control and/or rating
    bin.
  \item
    If one model performs worse than another but the better-performing
    model fails to pass for stability, then the `worse', more-stable
    model should be preferred.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  For clustering specifically, select solutions by stability and
  interpretability.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Candidate clusterings should be preferred when feature profiles are
    distinct and assignments are reasonably stable across adjacent
    months or within rating strata.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Because no single framework is sufficient for selecting between all
models tested, a large portion of the model-selection process will
instead be covered in Section~\ref{sec-results}.\footnote{As a note, I
  heavily suspect I'll be reworking this section before the final draft
  of this paper.}
